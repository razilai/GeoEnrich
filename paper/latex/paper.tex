\begin{abstract}
  We present GeoEnrich-NYC, a text-tabular dataset of 14,990 New York City Airbnb listings for nightly-price regression,
  in which each listing's coordinates are replaced by an LLM-generated description of its surroundings,
  built from Overture Maps places data.
  Evaluated with the MulTaBench curation pipeline, the dataset passes both curation criteria on all five learners:
  adding the description to the structured columns lowers RMSE on log price by 0.059--0.075,
  and fine-tuning the text encoder on the target lowers it by a further 0.002--0.007.
  The enrichment pipeline can be applied to other datasets with latitude-longitude coordinates,
  though we evaluate it on a single city.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Accommodation datasets (Airbnb, Booking.com) often include latitude-longitude coordinates
of listings.
The location of a listing can be leveraged as a significant indicator for price prediction
regression tasks, though the coordinates themselves say little about what surrounds a listing.
To solve this problem, traditional ML regression models engineer location features (e.g., spatial clustering)
to leverage this potentially informative feature.

In addition, the surroundings of a listing can often provide signal regarding its price. For example:
It is reasonable to assume \citep{rosen1974hedonic, wang2017price} that Airbnb listings in downtown Manhattan, where there is a high density
of restaurants and tourist attractions, will be pricier than listings in the outskirts of the city---where few
or no amenities can be found.

We therefore hypothesize that a natural-language description of a listing's
surroundings will improve models' fit in a nightly-price prediction regression task.

To achieve this, we leverage the given latitude-longitude coordinates from an existing Airbnb listings dataset
and enrich them with geospatial data by querying Overture Maps places data and enriching each listing with
business density information of its surroundings. We then use an LLM to transform the
numerical density information into a description of each listing's surroundings.

Our goal is a dataset that passes the MulTaBench \citep{arazi2026multabench} text-tabular curation criteria,
which GeoEnrich-NYC does on all five learners (Section~\ref{sec:results}).


\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Text-tabular curation.} MulTaBench \citep{arazi2026multabench} admits a text-tabular dataset only if,
across a committee of tabular learners, joint frozen text-tabular inputs improve on both unimodal baselines,
and target-aware representations (TAR), as in TabSTAR \citep{arazi2025tabstar}, improve on frozen ones.
We follow its protocol, with the one adaptation described in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
Earlier text-tabular benchmarks include Airbnb price datasets \citep{shi2021multimodal}; MulTaBench's curation
rejected both Airbnb datasets it evaluated (Melbourne Airbnb, 1/5 learners; Airbnb Seattle, 0/5).

\paragraph{LLM enrichment.} Large language models are increasingly used as components of data pipelines,
not only as predictors. Previous studies have leveraged modern LLMs
to generate a natural-language description of tabular features \citep{hegselmann2023tabllm}.
Closest to our pipeline, GeoLLM \citep{manvi2024geollm} prompts an LLM with coordinates and nearby OpenStreetMap places
to predict location-level indicators, including home values. We became aware of it only after building our pipeline,
which differs in withholding the coordinates from the LLM and leaving prediction to tabular learners.


\section{Dataset origins and curation methodology}

\subsection{Dataset and sources}

Inside Airbnb is a project that provides quarterly data and advocacy
about Airbnb's impact on residential communities; its data is freely available
online under a CC BY 4.0 licence. We used their New York City listings snapshot \citep{insideairbnbnyc}, scraped 14--23 June 2026.

Overture Maps Foundation is a non-profit organization that provides free and open-source maps data.
We used DuckDB to query the
places theme (licensed under CDLA-Permissive-2.0) of Overture release \texttt{2026-08-19.0} \citep{overtureplaces} for businesses and points of interest in our
desired city, and performed a join by proximity for each listing, with distances measured in a metric projection (UTM zone 18N).

\subsection{Pipeline}

\paragraph{Data cleaning.} After acquiring the original dataset from ``Inside Airbnb'', we perform a standard
feature engineering pipeline: columns that do not partake in the regression task are dropped,
the schema is defined and enforced, and features such as ``number of rooms'' and ``number of baths''
are extracted from semi-structured text fields using simple regex rules. Listings with missing or non-positive prices are dropped,
as are listings missing coordinates or more than 25\% of their features.
In addition, the target nightly-price column of the final version of the dataset is log-transformed: $\text{target} = \log(1 + \text{price})$.
Of the 30,259 listings in the snapshot, 21,155 survive these filters (Table~\ref{tab:attrition}); we randomly sample 15,000 of them (seed 0),
each with 8 feature columns (six numerical, one boolean, and one categorical) besides the target. The schema and feature distributions are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:data-card} (Appendix~\ref{app:data-card}).

\paragraph{Geospatial enrichment.} We query Overture for all places within the listings' bounding box (padded by $\approx 2$\,km)
that have a confidence score of at least 0.6 and belong to one of our selected categories.
We apply a join such that each listing coordinate is assigned all POIs (points of interest) in a 450-meter radius
(roughly 570 meters of walking on the Manhattan street grid), saved in JSON format.
The 10 listings with no POI within that radius are dropped, leaving 14,990 listings.
The original latitude-longitude columns are dropped by design: location reaches the models only through the text column,
so the structured columns carry no location information.

\paragraph{Aggregation.} Each JSON column describing nearby POIs is reduced down to a simple count,
and the distance to the nearest place, according to a list of 20 predefined buckets (for example: restaurants, parks, ground transport, and nightlife venues).
Those categories are curated from the level-2 categories of the official Overture hierarchical taxonomy.
We rank each listing's count in every category against the citywide distribution of that count over all listings, and replace the count-buckets of each listing with its relative percentile.
The ranking uses no price information, but is computed over all listings, including those later used for testing.
Popular POIs (landmarks)---77 curated attractions such as the Empire State Building, the Statue of Liberty, and Times Square---are matched
within 800 meters of each listing and not reduced down to their categories, as they potentially carry stronger neighbourhood signal,
which is also why they are matched over a wider radius.

\paragraph{LLM descriptive summary.} By the end of the aggregation stage, each row has a JSON object containing the listing's
relative density of each category in its 450-meter surrounding radius and a list of popular tourist attractions nearby,
including their exact distance. We take a prompt blueprint and structure the JSON summaries, preparing them for an LLM API call:
percentiles are phrased as word bands (e.g., ``far more than most neighbourhoods''), and only the three nearest landmarks are kept, with their distance phrased as a band (e.g., ``a short walk from'').
We send batch requests to an LLM (temperature 0.4), processing all 14,990 rows independently in three batches. The LLM describes
the surroundings and is not given the coordinates, exact counts, exact distances, or listing price, to prevent leakage.
Figure~\ref{fig:row-to-description} traces one listing through these steps.

Multiple LLMs were tested on subsets of the dataset; Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite \citep{gemini35flashlite} was chosen for its relative
lack of hallucinations on those subsets, generation speed, batch-request availability, and lack of reasoning.
The prompt was refined over 16 variants, tested on a subset of $\approx 2{,}000$ rows, before the final variant was fixed.

The released dataset thus pairs the structured columns with a single text column, \texttt{surroundings\_summary}
(statistics in Appendix~\ref{app:text-stats}).
The code, prompts, word-band rules, and landmark list are available at \url{https://github.com/razilai/GeoEnrich}.
The dataset is available on Hugging Face (\placeholder{link}) in MulTaBench's format (typed \texttt{data.parquet} and \texttt{metadata.json})
under CC BY 4.0, attributing Inside Airbnb and the Overture Maps Foundation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

With the prompt fixed, we evaluate GeoEnrich-NYC once with the MulTaBench benchmark, run from its current repository
(\texttt{master} at commit \texttt{d88821d}) with no change to its evaluation code; the dataset was added to its registry
and served from a local Hugging Face cache.
The curation committee consists of five learners: two GBDTs, LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost \citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost},
the MLP ensemble TabM \citep{gorishniy2024tabm}, and the tabular foundation models TabPFNv2 \citep{hollmann2025tabpfn} and
TabPFNv2.5 \citep{grinsztajn2025tabpfn25}.
Each is trained under four conditions: structured columns only, the surroundings description only (text-only),
structured columns with frozen E5-small \citep{wang2022e5} text embeddings (joint frozen), and structured columns with
E5-small embeddings fine-tuned on the target with low-rank adaptation \citep[LoRA;][]{hu2022lora} (joint TAR).
Every condition runs on the benchmark's six splits of TabArena's \citep{erickson2025tabarena} 67/33 scheme
($\approx 9{,}993$ training and $\approx 4{,}997$ test rows per split).
The metric is the RMSE on the log-price scale, $\log(1 + \text{price})$, as reported by the current repository (the MulTaBench paper uses $R^2$); lower is better.

A learner shows \emph{joint signal} when its mean joint-frozen RMSE is lower than both its structured and
its text-only RMSE by more than $0.001$, and \emph{TAR gain} when its mean joint-TAR RMSE is lower than its
joint-frozen RMSE by more than $0.001$ (means rounded to three decimals before differencing).
The dataset is eligible when at least 3 of the 5 learners show both.
We adapt the acceptance criterion of \citet[App.~A.3]{arazi2026multabench} to RMSE rather than $R^2$;
since the current repository no longer includes it, we compute it with our own code.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the results.
Adding the surroundings description to the structured columns lowers RMSE for every learner,
by $0.059$--$0.075$ ($11.8$--$15.3\%$ relative to the structured baseline), so all five learners show joint signal.
The description alone is a weak predictor: text-only RMSE ($0.710$--$0.726$) is well above the structured baseline
for every learner, which suggests the text complements the listing attributes, supplying the location information
they lack by design, rather than substituting for them.
Fine-tuning the encoder on the target lowers RMSE further for all five learners, so all five show TAR gain and the
dataset passes the MulTaBench curation criterion with 5/5 learners on both checks.
The TAR gain is small, however ($0.002$--$0.007$), and of the same order as the split-to-split standard deviation;
joint TAR beats joint frozen on 27 of the 30 learner--split pairs (6/6 splits for LightGBM, TabPFNv2 and TabPFNv2.5;
5/6 for CatBoost; 4/6 for TabM; per-learner paired differences in Appendix~\ref{app:paired}).
The best configuration, TabPFNv2.5 with joint TAR, reaches an RMSE of $0.412$, a $15.9\%$ reduction over its structured baseline.


\section{Conclusion, limitations, and future work}

\subsection{Outcomes}

GeoEnrich-NYC passes both MulTaBench curation criteria on all five learners.
Most of the gain comes from adding the surroundings description to the structured columns;
the further gain from fine-tuning the encoder is an order of magnitude smaller and comparable to split-to-split variation.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}

\paragraph{Evaluation.} It is unclear what the main driver of the price signal is in each listing's surroundings description.
It is possible that it is derived from the explicit naming of popular landmarks (present in the evidence of 42.6\% of listings),
or perhaps by the overall busyness band and the category contrasts.
The pipeline already builds two censored arms that separate these sources --- one keeping only the landmark names found in each summary,
and one replacing each landmark name with ``a well-known landmark'' --- but they were not evaluated in this release.
The prompt was selected on rows that are also part of the released dataset, and was not re-validated on held-out rows.
MulTaBench's random splits also place nearby listings, which share near-identical surroundings, in both training and test sets
(12,594 distinct rendered evidence blocks for 14,990 listings), which may inflate the gains relative to unseen areas.
Finally, Overture's places coverage is likely uneven across the city, so a ``quiet'' band may partly reflect sparse data.

\paragraph{Application.} The pipeline needs only latitude-longitude coordinates, so it can be applied to other datasets,
including Inside Airbnb's other cities, but we have evaluated it on a single city.
The prompts are worded for hospitality and real-estate tasks, and the manually curated landmark list must be rebuilt for each city.

\paragraph{Future work.} Future work could evaluate the pipeline beyond hospitality price regression. Since coordinates alone
could carry the same or a stronger location signal, it could also rerun the four conditions with coordinates kept as
structured columns and test whether TAR still beats joint frozen.
