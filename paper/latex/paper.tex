\begin{abstract}
  We present GeoEnrich-NYC, a text-tabular dataset of 14,990 New York City Airbnb listings for nightly-price regression,
  in which each listing's coordinates are replaced by an LLM-generated description of its surroundings,
  built from Overture Maps places data.
  Evaluated with the MulTaBench curation pipeline, the dataset passes both curation criteria on all five learners:
  adding the description to the structured columns lowers RMSE on log price by 0.059--0.075,
  and fine-tuning the text encoder on the target lowers it by a further 0.002--0.007.
  The enrichment pipeline can be applied to other datasets with latitude-longitude coordinates.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Text-tabular benchmarks require datasets in which the text column carries signal that the structured columns lack.
MulTaBench \citep{arazi2026multabench} defines a curation criterion: a dataset is admitted only if,
across a committee of tabular learners, adding frozen text embeddings to the structured columns beats both unimodal baselines,
and target-aware representations (TAR), text embeddings fine-tuned on the target, beat frozen ones.
Neither Airbnb dataset MulTaBench evaluated passed, even though both include free-text listing fields.

Accommodation listings carry a signal that their text fields rarely describe: location.
A listing in downtown Manhattan, surrounded by restaurants and tourist attractions, plausibly commands
a higher nightly price than a comparable one in an outer neighbourhood with few amenities nearby;
hedonic pricing theory formalizes this intuition by treating location as one of the characteristics a price reflects
(Section~\ref{sec:related}).
Yet datasets scraped from platforms such as Airbnb and Booking.com record location only as latitude-longitude coordinates,
which say nothing directly about what surrounds a listing.
We ask whether a natural-language description of those surroundings can supply this signal,
that is, whether adding it to the structured columns lowers held-out error in nightly-price regression.

We build GeoEnrich-NYC to test this. Starting from Inside Airbnb's New York City listings \citep{insideairbnbnyc},
we join each listing to the Overture Maps places \citep{overtureplaces} within 450 meters,
reduce them to citywide percentile ranks over 20 place categories plus the nearest of 77 curated landmarks,
phrase these as word bands, and prompt an LLM to describe the surroundings (Figure~\ref{fig:row-to-description}).
We use text rather than adding the counts as numeric columns because the dataset targets text-tabular learning,
and the word bands keep only what stands out against the rest of the city.
Two design choices keep the evaluation clean: the LLM never sees coordinates, exact counts, exact distances, or price,
so the description cannot copy the target directly; and the coordinate columns are dropped, so location reaches the models
only through the text (whether the text adds to coordinates is left to future work).

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GeoEnrich-NYC}, a text-tabular regression dataset of 14,990 listings with 8 structured columns,
    one generated text column, and log nightly price as the target, released under CC BY 4.0 in MulTaBench's format.
  \item \textbf{A geospatial enrichment pipeline} that needs only latitude-longitude coordinates and withholds
    coordinates, exact measurements, and price from the LLM.
  \item \textbf{A MulTaBench evaluation} in which the dataset passes both curation criteria on all five learners.
    (Section~\ref{sec:results}; limits in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{itemize}


\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Text-tabular curation.} We follow the MulTaBench protocol \citep{arazi2026multabench},
whose TAR condition builds on TabSTAR \citep{arazi2025tabstar}, with the one adaptation described in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
MulTaBench's curation rejected both Airbnb datasets it evaluated (Melbourne Airbnb, 1/5 learners; Airbnb Seattle, 0/5).

\paragraph{Location and LLMs in prediction.} Hedonic pricing theory \citep{rosen1974hedonic} models the price of a
differentiated good as a function of its characteristics, each carrying an implicit price.
For accommodation, location is one such characteristic; our surroundings description expresses it in text
rather than as coordinates.
GeoLLM \citep{manvi2024geollm} prompts an LLM with coordinates and nearby OpenStreetMap places
to predict location-level indicators, including home values, drawing on the LLM's own geographic knowledge.


\section{Dataset construction}

\subsection{Dataset and sources}

We use Inside Airbnb's New York City listings snapshot \citep{insideairbnbnyc}, scraped 14--23 June 2026 (CC BY 4.0),
and query, with DuckDB, the places theme of Overture Maps release \texttt{2026-08-19.0} \citep{overtureplaces} (CDLA-Permissive-2.0)
for businesses and points of interest (POIs) in the city.

\subsection{Pipeline}

The pipeline has four stages: data cleaning, geospatial enrichment, aggregation, and an LLM descriptive summary.

\paragraph{Data cleaning.} We drop columns that do not partake in the regression task and enforce a schema.
Listings with missing or non-positive prices are dropped,
as are listings missing coordinates or more than 25\% of their features.
Of the 30,259 listings in the snapshot, 21,155 survive these filters (Table~\ref{tab:attrition}); we randomly sample 15,000 of them,
each with 8 feature columns besides the target.
This size matches MulTaBench's per-split cap of 10,000 training and 5,000 test rows, so the benchmark uses every row.
Due to the skewed distribution of raw nightly-price - the target is log-transformed, $\log(1 + \text{price})$.
Table~\ref{tab:data-card} (Appendix~\ref{app:data-card}) summarizes the schema and feature distributions;
the same appendix describes column parsing and missing-value handling.

\paragraph{Geospatial enrichment.} We query Overture for all places within the listings' bounding box
that have a confidence score of at least 0.6 and belong to one of our selected categories.
We apply a join such that each listing coordinate is assigned all POIs in a 450-meter radius.
The 10 listings with no POI within that radius are dropped, leaving 14,990 listings.

\paragraph{Aggregation.} Each listing's nearby POIs are reduced to a count and the distance to the nearest place in each of 20 predefined categories
(for example: restaurants, parks, ground transport, and nightlife venues).
Those categories are curated from the level-2 categories of the official Overture hierarchical taxonomy.
We rank each listing's count in every category, and its total count over all categories, against the citywide distribution of that count over all listings,
and replace each count with its percentile.
The ranking uses no price information, but is computed over all listings, including those later used for testing.
Landmarks---77 curated attractions such as the Empire State Building, the Statue of Liberty, and Times Square---may carry
stronger neighbourhood signal, so they are kept by name rather than reduced to categories, and matched over a wider 800-meter radius.

\paragraph{LLM descriptive summary.} Each aggregated record is rendered as a short \emph{evidence block},
the only listing-specific input the LLM receives, in which word bands replace the numbers
(Figure~\ref{fig:row-to-description}, column~2): an overall busyness band (``very quiet'' to ``very busy''),
up to four category contrasts (citywide top or bottom 15\%, or a common category absent nearby),
at most one proximity cue within 150 meters (``steps away''), limited to telling place types such as parks and transit,
and up to three landmarks within 800 meters in walking-distance bands (``right by'' to ``about 10 minutes from'').
Typical categories are omitted, and the sections are shuffled per listing so the summaries do not inherit
the block's order.
The prompt asks for one or two neutral sentences that use only the given evidence and keep each comparison's strength.
We chose Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite \citep{gemini35flashlite} (\texttt{google/gemini-3.5-flash-lite}) over other LLMs
tested on subsets for its relative lack of hallucinations, speed, and low cost, and fixed the prompt after testing
16 variants on $\approx 2{,}000$ rows.
Each listing is summarized independently through OpenRouter's batch API at temperature 0.4;
generation is sampled, so reruns differ and the released text column is the reference.

The released dataset thus pairs the structured columns with a single text column, \texttt{surroundings\_summary}
(statistics in Appendix~\ref{app:text-stats}).
The code, prompts, word-band rules, and landmark list are available at \url{https://github.com/razilai/GeoEnrich}.
The dataset is available on Hugging Face at \url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/ElyR120/geoenrich-nyc} in MulTaBench's format (typed \texttt{data.parquet} and \texttt{metadata.json})
under CC BY 4.0, attributing Inside Airbnb and the Overture Maps Foundation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We evaluate GeoEnrich-NYC once with the MulTaBench benchmark.
The curation committee consists of five learners: two GBDTs, LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost \citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost},
the MLP ensemble TabM \citep{gorishniy2024tabm}, and the tabular foundation models TabPFNv2 \citep{hollmann2025tabpfn} and
TabPFNv2.5 \citep{grinsztajn2025tabpfn25}.
Each is trained under four conditions: structured columns only, the surroundings description only (text-only),
structured columns with frozen E5-small \citep{wang2022e5} text embeddings (joint frozen), and structured columns with
E5-small embeddings fine-tuned on the target with low-rank adaptation \citep[LoRA;][]{hu2022lora} (joint TAR).
Every condition runs on the benchmark's six splits of TabArena's \citep{erickson2025tabarena} 67/33 scheme
($\approx 9{,}993$ training and $\approx 4{,}997$ test rows per split).
The metric is the RMSE on the log-price scale, $\log(1 + \text{price})$, as reported by the current repository (the MulTaBench paper uses $R^2$); lower is better.

A learner shows \emph{joint signal} when its mean joint-frozen RMSE is lower than both its structured and
its text-only RMSE by more than $0.001$, and \emph{TAR gain} when its mean joint-TAR RMSE is lower than its
joint-frozen RMSE by more than $0.001$ (means rounded to three decimals before differencing).
The dataset is eligible when at least 3 of the 5 learners show both.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the results.
Adding the surroundings description to the structured columns lowers RMSE for every learner,
by $0.059$--$0.075$ ($11.8$--$15.3\%$ relative to the structured baseline), so all five learners show joint signal.
The description alone is a weak predictor: text-only RMSE ($0.710$--$0.726$) is well above the structured baseline
for every learner, which suggests the text complements the listing attributes, supplying the location information
they lack by design, rather than substituting for them.
Fine-tuning the encoder on the target lowers RMSE further for all five learners, so all five show TAR gain and the
dataset passes the MulTaBench curation criterion with 5/5 learners on both checks.
The TAR gain is small, however ($0.002$--$0.007$), and of the same order as the split-to-split standard deviation;
joint TAR beats joint frozen on 27 of the 30 learner--split pairs (per-learner paired differences in Appendix~\ref{app:paired}).
The best configuration, TabPFNv2.5 with joint TAR, reaches an RMSE of $0.412$, a $15.9\%$ reduction over its structured baseline.


\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Evaluation.} It is unclear what the main driver of the price signal is in each listing's surroundings description.
It is possible that it is derived from the explicit naming of popular landmarks (present in the evidence of 42.6\% of listings),
or perhaps by the overall busyness band and the category contrasts.
The pipeline already builds two censored arms that separate these sources --- one keeping only the landmark names found in each summary,
and one replacing each landmark name with ``a well-known landmark'' --- but they were not evaluated in this release.
The prompt was selected on rows that are also part of the released dataset, and was not re-validated on held-out rows.
MulTaBench's random splits also place nearby listings, which share near-identical surroundings, in both training and test sets
(12,594 distinct rendered evidence blocks for 14,990 listings), which may inflate the gains relative to unseen areas.
Finally, Overture's places coverage is likely uneven across the city, so a ``quiet'' band may partly reflect sparse data.

\paragraph{Application.} The pipeline needs only latitude-longitude coordinates, so it can be applied to other datasets,
including Inside Airbnb's other cities, but we have evaluated it on a single city.
The prompts are worded for hospitality and real-estate tasks, and the manually curated landmark list must be rebuilt for each city.

\paragraph{Future work.} Future work could evaluate the pipeline beyond hospitality price regression. Since coordinates alone
could carry the same or a stronger location signal, it could also rerun the four conditions with coordinates kept as
structured columns and test whether TAR still beats joint frozen.

\section{Evidence-block rules}
\label{app:evidence}

The evidence block has up to four sections, each built from the aggregated record with word bands in place of numbers:
(i)~an overall line gives the citywide quintile of the total place count, from ``very quiet'' through ``quiet'', ``average'', and ``busy'' to ``very busy'';
(ii)~a category is listed only if its percentile lies in the outer 15\% (at or above the 85th, with at least three places, or at or below the 15th),
as ``more than most neighbourhoods'' (``far more than most neighbourhoods'' from the 95th, ``more than almost anywhere in NYC'' from the 99th),
``fewer than most neighbourhoods'' (``very few'' up to the 5th), or ``none nearby'' for a category that at least 60\% of listings have nearby;
typical categories are omitted, and at most the four strongest contrasts are kept, one per level-1 category;
(iii)~at most one proximity cue marks the nearest park, museum, hotel, nightlife venue, historic site, or ground-transport service
(rail, transit, or taxi) within 150 meters as ``on the doorstep'' (up to 50 meters) or ``steps away'', skipping categories already listed in (ii);
(iv)~up to three nearest landmarks within 800 meters are named (otherwise the block states that none is nearby), with their distance phrased as ``right by'', ``a couple minutes from'', ``a short walk from'',
or ``about 10 minutes from'' (up to 100, 250, 500, and 800 meters, respectively).
These sections, and the contrasts in (ii), appear in a per-listing shuffled order (seeded, so reproducible),
so the summaries do not inherit a fixed evidence structure.
